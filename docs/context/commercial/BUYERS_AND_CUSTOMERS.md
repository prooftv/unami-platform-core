# Unami Foundation — Buyers, Customers and the Commercial Model

Constitutional reference. Read before any commercial conversation, pricing discussion,
partnership proposal, or presentation preparation.

This document exists because the distinction between who pays, who uses, and who benefits
is not obvious in the Unami model — and getting it wrong produces the wrong commercial strategy.

---

## The Core Insight

**The institution using the platform is not always the institution paying for it.**

A Traditional Council uses Umkhandlu. A municipality, mine, or contractor pays for a
coordination service that the council's platform enables. The council is the customer.
The municipality is the buyer. The community is the beneficiary.

This is not a complication. It is the commercial model.

---

## The Four Roles

Every Unami commercial relationship involves up to four distinct roles.
These are commercial roles — not rigid categories. One organisation can occupy multiple roles.

### 1. The Buyer / Payer
The organisation, institution, department, business, sponsor or funding partner that
purchases or finances a service. May pay for deployment, onboarding, training, support,
maintenance, or a defined operational service.

### 2. The Customer / Contracting Partner
The organisation or authorised representative that enters into an arrangement with Unami,
defines the requirements, agrees on responsibilities, and receives the contracted service.
Often this is the buyer — but a sponsor can pay on a customer's behalf.

### 3. The Users / Operating Partners
The people and organisations who use the infrastructure in their everyday activities.
Council secretaries, Izinduna, school principals, team coaches, community liaison officers.

### 4. The Beneficiaries / Wider Market
The learners, players, families, residents, community organisations and other stakeholders
who gain from improved coordination, access to information, capability, or services.
Some can also become direct customers.

---

## The Two Commercial Layers

Every Unami operating partnership has two commercial layers.

### Layer 1 — Operating Partnership Services
The relationship with the institution or community organisation:
- Initial setup, deployment and configuration
- Onboarding and training
- Ongoing technical support and maintenance
- Content, presentation and operational assistance
- Agreed programme delivery and stakeholder coordination

### Layer 2 — Foundation-Operated Infrastructure and Services
The services Unami itself operates or coordinates on behalf of the institution:
- Statutory notices (EIA, SPLUMA, mining, liquor, estate)
- Infrastructure project coordination and milestone tracking
- CSR campaign management and certified impact reporting
- SMME and EPWP coordination services
- Commissioned stakeholder coordination and operational outputs

**Revenue from Layer 2 flows to Unami Foundation — not to the institution.**
The institution retains governance authority. Unami operates the commercial coordination layer.
External stakeholders pay for defined services connected to the institution's activities.

---

## The Khathide Resolution — The Constitutional Proof

The Khathide Traditional Council resolution (adopted at a duly constituted council meeting)
formally establishes this model. Key clauses:

- **§2:** The Council retains full governance authority over all published content.
  The platform operates under Council mandate, not independently.
  All data belongs to the Council. The Council pays no fees for platform access or operation.
- **§2:** Revenue from external parties (developers, attorneys, businesses) flows to
  Unami Foundation as the platform operator.
- **§5:** The platform operator does not make governance decisions, does not adjudicate
  disputes, does not represent any contractor, municipality, or political party.
  Records, preserves, organises, and presents information only.

This resolution is the template for every subsequent council deployment.
The same structure applies to every Traditional Council in Amajuba District and beyond.

## The Economic Buyer Distinction

The economic buyer is the person or organisation with the budget and authority to approve
expenditure. Identifying the economic buyer is critical when the direct user cannot afford
the service independently.

| Market | Direct Customer | Possible Economic Buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional governance | Traditional Council | Council, municipality, mine, COGTA programme |
| Education | School or institution | School budget, sponsor, education body |
| Community sport | Team or academy | Academy, sponsor, development programme |
| Infrastructure coordination | Municipality / PMU | Project budget (MIG, WSIG, RBIG) |
| Statutory compliance | Attorney / developer | Client disbursements |
| CSR campaigns | Corporate sponsor | CSR budget |
| Drone training | Learner or cohort | Learner, family, sponsor, commissioning org |
| Timber preservation | Timber owner / project | Builder, contractor, timber business |

## What This Means for Commercial Conversations

### When talking to a Traditional Council:
- They are the customer and operating partner — not the buyer
- They pay nothing for platform access or operation (free tier / pilot model)
- Their governance authority is the asset that makes the platform valuable to everyone else
- The conversation is about adoption, not price

### When talking to a municipality or PMU:
- They are a potential buyer for Layer 2 coordination services
- They have procurement processes — quotation or tender
- The conversation is about the service, the output, and the reporting value
- Reference: Newcastle PMU relationship, Buffalo River project

### When talking to a mine or contractor:
- They are a potential buyer for community engagement records, SLP governance, CSR campaigns
- They have direct commercial budgets — no tender required for smaller amounts
- The conversation is about institutional memory, audit trail, and community trust
- Reference: mining presentation at `/presentation/mining`

### When talking to an attorney or developer:
- They are a per-transaction buyer for statutory notices
- The conversation is about cost vs. print media, speed, and proof of publication
- No ongoing relationship required — transactional

### When talking to COGTA:
- They are a potential programme buyer for district-wide deployment
- The conversation is about governance compliance, audit trails, and institutional resilience
- Reference: COGTA presentation at `/presentation/cogta`

## The Sustainability Model

The Foundation's sustainability combines:

1. **Institutional service agreements** — deployment, training, support, maintenance
2. **Commissioned services** — statutory notices, project coordination, CSR campaigns
3. **Sponsored access** — where the direct customer lacks funds, a sponsor pays
4. **District or provincial programmes** — COGTA or district municipality funds multi-council deployment
5. **Grants and partnerships** — for shared infrastructure and early-stage community programmes
6. **Enterprise revenue** — drone training, timber preservation (when operational)

The commercial quotations must account for the real cost of delivery:
platform subscriptions, infrastructure, deployment, training, support, maintenance,
data, integration, and operational overhead — with contingency.
A deployed pilot or free-tier service is not evidence that its ongoing delivery costs are zero.

## The Answer When Someone Asks

**Who are Unami Foundation's buyers and customers?**

Unami Foundation serves institutions, community organisations and commercial partners
through a portfolio of digital infrastructure, operating partnerships and practical services.

Our direct customers include Traditional Councils, schools, community-recognised soccer teams
and academies. We also target institutional and commercial buyers — municipalities, mines,
engineering contractors, attorneys and other organisations that require defined coordination,
reporting, training or technical services.

Our buyers are the organisations or authorised individuals who approve and pay for those
services. In some cases the customer pays directly; in others, a sponsor, development
programme or institutional partner finances the service on their behalf.

The people who benefit are often broader than the paying customer: learners, players,
families, community members, governance participants and local stakeholders.

Our Technology for Good programme focuses on three operating partnerships: Unami Schools,
Unami Sports and Umkhandlu. Across these, Unami provides relevant infrastructure,
implementation, training, technical support, maintenance and programme coordination,
while participating institutions retain their own authority and administrative responsibilities.

**Our sustainability model is to combine institutional service agreements, commissioned
services, sponsorship and appropriate enterprise revenue so that the infrastructure and
support behind community-led activity can be maintained over time.**
